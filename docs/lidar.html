5  LiDAR data and its derivatives

5.1 A bit of history

Remote sensing encourages a wider, regional perspective, seeing sites within a landscape and recognizing the pathways that link them. (P. Sheets and Sever 1988)

Probably the first use of LiDAR (at least published) was around 1988 in dense vegetation of rain forest area in Costa Rica at Arenal volcano area, and later in Ceren area, El Salvador. In the research an NASA airborne oceanographic LiDAR was used along with other sensing devices: side-looking airborne synthetic aperture radar and thermal infrared multispectral scanner (P. Sheets and Sever 1988; P. D. Sheets 1991). They wrote:

The laser beam pulses to the ground 400 times per second, striking the surface every three and half inches, and bounces back to its source. In most cases, the beam bounces off the top of the vegetation cover and off the ground surface; the difference between the two gives information on forest height, or even the height of grass in pastures. As the lidar passes over an eroded footpath that still affects the topography, the pathway’s indentation is recorded by the laser beam.

The first impression of results obtained from LiDAR measurements was a bit skeptical. Although they admit the possibility to reach under the trees canopy and therefore extract the DTM, the issue faced was linear measurement and therefore a need to fly a large number of parallel flight lines to generate microtopographic data. The another problem highlighted was impossibility to distinguish natural erosional channels from cultural erosional features like prehistoric footpaths — see P. Sheets and Sever (1991); McKee and Sever (1994); McKee, Sever, and Sheets (1994).

Ten years have passed. Ten years without any mention of LiDAR measurements being used in archaeology. But it doesn’t mean, that nothing happens in meantime. LiDAR was used to measure the altimetry of Greenland Ice Sheet (Ferraro and Swift 1995), to measure the vegetation itself (forests), to capture and extract the buildings or even power lines and poles (Ackermann 1999). Firsts algorithms for LiDAR points filtration and creation of DTM started to appear: the one made by Kraus and Pfeifer (1998) based on linear prediction (interpolation) and another one, based on TIN models by Axelsson (2000).

A quarter of a century ago, in 2001 Holden (2001) described the principles of LiDAR and Motkin (2001) did the assessment of LiDAR data in archeaeological work based on data in form of xyz ASCII grid covering 71 square kilometers. The data was then converted to 2 m resolution DTM and examined. It was concluded that LiDAR has considerable potential in archaeology. That was the first time when a shaded relief models of hillfort and other earthwork enclosure was published.

5.2 DTM vs DEM

There is many definitions of digital models: DSM — digital surface model, DHM (height), DTM (terrain) etc. We will use the definitions defined by El-Sheimy, Valeo, and Habib (2005):

The two most widely used terms are DEM and DTM. These terms are often used synonymously. Usually, the acronym DTM refers to the altitude of the ground itself. On the contrary, a DEM includes the maximum altitude everywhere (including roofs of buildings and tops of trees).

In next paragraphs we will use: DTM — for terrain model (bare ground + water) and DEM — for an elevation model, including buildings and trees. Figure 5.1 shows the difference.

5.3 LiDAR processing

The main use of LiDAR data is to create very detailed DTM – a very common approach is to create DTM with resolution of 1 m, but it might be reasonable to go with higher resolution (at cost of data processing time and required storage). LiDAR data processing for DTM generation consist of the four main steps: error filtering, interpolation and/or resampling, filtration (classification) of the ground points and finally DTM generation through interpolation of the DTM surface based on those filtered ground points.

  1. Error filtering – LiDAR point outliers are often randomly distributed in the dataset. Outliers may be caused by airplanes, birds, multi-path reflections or the sensor itself. These points have unreasonably high or low elevation values and must be removed during preprocessing. Those outliers can be removed using a elevation histogram examination: after setting up the elevation thresholds the lowest and highest tails can be removed from the distribution. The remaining outliers can be found using the minimum height difference of each point with respect to all its neighbors (Silván-Cárdenas and Wang 2006; Meng et al. 2009). Kobler et al. (2007) computed the vertical displacement of the negative outliers to the average elevation of its neighboring points in X–Y plane to filter out those points.

  2. Interpolation and resampling – some of the ground filtering techniques requires a raster dataset to be created form LiDAR points as a intermediate step (Hyyppä et al. 2000; Wack and Wimmer 2002; Nardinocchi, Forlani, and Zingaretti 2003; Lloyd and Atkinson 2006; Forlani, Nardinocchi, and Scaioni 2006; Zheng et al. 2007).

  3. Ground filtering – is the process of separating ground from non-ground points. Several methods are used: directional scanning, morphology-, interpolation- or segmentation-based.

  4. Generation of DTM – by interpolation of the points identified in previous steps. Examples of the methods are: Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW), AMLE, kriging

The evaluations of different algorithms developed for filtering and interpolation of LiDAR data were performed by, among others, Sithole and Vosselman (2004), Aguilar et al. (2005), Aguilar and Mills (2008), Bater and Coops (2009), Heritage et al. (2009) and Meng, Currit, and Zhao (2010).

5.4 Land surface parameters — slope, aspect, and other derivatives

Those land surface parameters are derived directly from DTM, and usually they are divided to “local” and “regional”. While the “local” can be calculated usually using 3x3 window, the “regional” one includes additional parts of DTM apart from the exact area for which parameters are to be calculated (Florinsky 1998; Olaya 2009). Typical examples of local primary land surface parameters include slope, aspect, profile and tangential curvatures; non‐local primary land surface parameters include flow path length, proximity to nearest ridgeline or channel, and upslope contributing area. Wilson (2018) provided the list of 68 most frequently used parameters.

Visually analyzing and interpretation of this number of images can be difficult, expensive and time consuming. But there is a help of relative old technique used in statistic to reduce the dimensions.

PCA is a mathematical procedure transforming a number of correlated variables into a smaller number of uncorrelated variables, called “principal components”. Used to image processing, it allows to reduce the number of image bands (in our case: raster layers) but retain most of the original variability in the multi-layer data. The first principal component accounts for as much of the variability in the data as possible, and each succeeding component accounts for as much of the remaining variability as possible. For detailed description of the technique refer to works of Mather and Koch (2011) and Gonzalez and Woods (2019).

Filtering out the large scale topography and emphasizes micro-reliefs is another possible method. While Doneus and Briese (2006) used subtraction of resampled (aggregated) DTM to emphasize the local relief, Humme, Lindenbergh, and Sueur (2006) used the geostatistical kriging method to show Celtic fields on the plateau close to Doorwerth in The Netherlands. Another, less computationally expensive, workflow called Local Relief Model was provided by Hesse (2010). It consist of following steps:

  1. DTM is produced from vegatation-filtered LiDAR points

  2. Low pass filter over DTM - to smooth it. The kernel size of the low-pass filter determines the spatial scale of features captured by LRM.

  3. Substraction this smoothed elevation model from original DTM

  4. Extraction of the zero-metre contour lines from the difference DTM

  5. Extraction of point elevations from DEM along the zero-metre contour lines and interpolation of new DEM

  6. Substraction of the purged DEM from the original DEM

McCoy, Asner, and Graves (2011) described a procedure called slope contrast mapping, where slope is first calculated and then classified in a raster surface as flat, low, or high. Then the raster is vectorized into polygons representing microtopographical regions that can be used to easily distinguish certain types of construction forms from the natural landscape. Lasaponara, Coluzzi, and Masini (2011) presented the data processing chain along with the threshold-based algorithm, which was devised for the classification of ground and non-ground points and for the detection of archaeological features. The used method prooved that the devised data processing provides highly satisfactory results for different landscapes and enables the detection of archaeological microtopographic relief in sparsely as well as in densely vegetated areas.

6.1 Sky view factor

Sky-view factor is a relatively old technique of topographic analysis (see tu znaleźć kto i gdzie używał) but was adapted in archeology to address the directional problems of hill-shading. It give advantages over other techniques because it reveals small relief features while preserving the perception of general topography (Zakšek, Oštir, and Kokalj 2011; Kokalj, Zakšek, and Oštir 2011). SVF is a geophysical parameter — it measures the portion of the sky visible from a certain point.
